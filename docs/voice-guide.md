# How Crystalina speaks and sells

The standard every customer-facing line is checked against before it ships.
It is built from research and from the people who sold by writing, not from
taste, and each rule names its source. CLAUDE.md states the voice; this
page is the test.

## 1. Say the fact. Cut the adjective.

Claude Hopkins, writing in 1923 from measured mail-order results, found that
"best in the world" and "lowest price in existence" are discounted by the
reader as the expected claim, while a specific figure is taken at full
weight. Ogilvy built on him: specifics, case studies and testimonials sold,
and the headline carried most of the result. Nielsen Norman measured it on
the web: against a promotional control, objective language alone was 27
percent more usable, concise text at half the word count 58 percent, and
the three together, concise, scannable and objective, 124 percent. Readers
pay a cost to filter hype before they reach the fact.

- A number with a source beats any adjective. "Lead reduced 99 percent under
  laboratory conditions" is copy. "Ultra-pure" is noise.
- No superlatives. No "best", "ultimate", "premium", "revolutionary".
- One idea per paragraph, the conclusion first. Seventy-nine percent of
  readers scan; the opening words of a paragraph are the only ones many see.
- Half the words you first wrote.
- The headline carries most of the result. Ogilvy: on average five times as
  many people read the headline as the body, and when it is written you have
  spent eighty cents of your dollar. A headline states the benefit or the
  fact; never a pun, never a riddle, never a tease.
- Long copy is welcome when every sentence is information. Ogilvy sold by
  mail with facts, cases and named testimonials: the more you tell, the more
  you sell, provided what you tell is true and specific. Percentages, time
  elapsed, dollars saved.

## 2. Never grade your own products in public.

A shop that says "three tiers" has told the customer which of its products
are the lesser ones and invited them to shop the ladder. Positioning is
done with the range, the photograph and the price, never with a label on
the page. Kapferer and Bastien's rules for luxury hold here even though
Crystalina is premium rather than luxury: the brand does not compare itself,
does not respond to the customer with categories of worth, and lets the
object carry its own standing.

- Describe who a system is for and where it goes, never how good it is
  relative to the others. "Under the sink, on the counter, or for the whole
  house." Never "entry", "mid", "flagship tier".
- Budget is said as inclusion, in passing: "for every home and every
  budget". Never as cheapness, never as a ranking.
- The brand speaks as one voice about one standard. If a system is good
  enough to sell, it is good enough to describe without apology.
- Price holds. Kapferer and Bastien, in their own anti-laws: never discount
  to answer a slow week, and let price follow the object rather than the
  object follow a price. For a premium brand that means no "deal",
  "clearance" or countdown language on a system, and a struck-through price
  only when it was the real price for a real period.
- Scarcity is real or it is absent. Their anti-law 7, in their words: the
  greater the inaccessibility, actual or virtual, the greater the desire;
  but rarity is to be distributed skilfully and only where no real shortage
  exists. We do not manufacture urgency. "Sold out" is a fact we state
  plainly, not a device.
- The brand sets the experience of buying. Their rule against letting the
  channel define the brand is why the listing, the product page and the
  receipt are ours in every detail, and why nothing on them looks borrowed
  from a marketplace.

## 3. One idea the customer can remember, repeated the same way.

Sharp's work at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute: brands grow by being easy to
notice, recall and recognise at the moment of buying, and that comes from
distinctive assets kept consistent, not from cleverness. For Crystalina the
assets are the mark, the navy and cyan, Playfair on titles, the photograph
of a New York kitchen, and one sentence: clean water at home, for the
people who care what goes into their bodies, from people who know New York
water.

- The same words for the same things everywhere: "system", "filter",
  "fitted", "Water Facts". Never a synonym for variety.
- New York water is the reason we know what we are doing. It is the proof,
  not the headline.

## 4. Proof the customer can check, or nothing.

Nielsen Norman: credibility rises with good writing and falls with claims
the reader cannot verify. Cialdini's social proof only works when the
proof is real; a rating with no reviews behind it, or a count no one can
trace, is a liability the day someone asks. Baymard's listing research adds
the mechanics: a rating average shown without its count is distrusted, and
a listing that lacks the facts a shopper needs sends them pogo-sticking
into product pages and out of the site.

- Every number on the site has a source we can produce: a laboratory
  report, a policy, a product specification, a real review count. No
  source, no number.
- Ratings appear only with their count, and only when there are reviews.
- Testimonials carry a name and a place and are real people who agreed.

## 5. Price with a real anchor, and frame the gain honestly.

Tversky and Kahneman's anchoring effect is strong and automatic; Nielsen
Norman's only guardrail is that an anchor reflect real information. Our
anchor is the cost of the water a system replaces, which the Water Facts
page already calculates for a household of four. A system that "pays for
itself in bottles never bought" is an honest frame only while that chart
exists and its arithmetic is shown.

- Show a struck-through price only when it was the real price.
- Free delivery states its threshold every time: "over $99".
- Never "save up to". Say what it costs and what it replaces.

## 6. Fewer, clearer choices.

Iyengar and Lepper's jam table is overstated as a law but holds where it
was measured: when options are hard to compare and the buyer is unsure,
more choice means fewer purchases. Baymard: a listing must carry price,
title or type, a thumbnail, rating with count, and the variation that
decides the choice, plus one to three category facts, so the shopper can
decide which to open without opening all of them.

- Six categories, one filter bar, four across. Nothing else on the listing.
- The one grey line beneath a product says the fact that decides it:
  capacity, where it goes, or that it is sold out.
- The quiz exists so a person who is unsure is asked five questions rather
  than shown seventeen products.

## 7. Write it the way a careful person talks.

Ogilvy's memo to his agency: natural language, short words, short sentences,
short paragraphs, no jargon, say what you want the reader to do. CLAUDE.md
adds the house rules: no em or en dashes, no emoji, no note to the designer
in visible text, addressed to the reader.

- A call to action starts with a verb and names the outcome: "Shop the
  Systems", "Find Your Filter", "Request this manual".
- An empty state says what this is, why it is empty, and how to start.
- An error says what happened, why, and what to do.

## 8. Who we are talking to

Mid-income New Yorkers, the many rather than the few, who care what their
household drinks. The numbers that shape every line: the median household
income in the city is $80,483 (Census Bureau QuickFacts, 2020 to 2024, in
2024 dollars), and 67.3 percent of households rent (NYU Furman Center,
from the 2024 American Community Survey). Two thirds of the people reading
the shop cannot touch the plumbing, which is why the countertop system is
never an afterthought and why "renting or owning" appears where it does.

- Price is said as value against something they already pay for, never as
  a luxury and never as a bargain.
- A renter has to see, within one screen, that there is a system for them.
- Nothing on the site assumes a car, a basement, a landlord's permission
  or a second home.

## 9. The reserve: selling to the many

The premium shelf above decides. When it does not settle a question, the
people who sold to the whole market are the second opinion, in this order.

- **Sharp, How Brands Grow.** Brands grow by reaching more buyers, most of
  them light buyers, not by deepening loyalty; be easy to notice, recall and
  recognise, with distinctive assets kept the same for years. For us: the
  same mark, colours, type and sentence on every surface, and reach before
  cleverness.
- **Binet and Field, The Long and the Short of It (IPA, 2013, 996 cases).**
  The most effective campaigns put about 60 percent into brand building and
  40 percent into activation, and emotional campaigns outperform rational
  ones over the long term, almost twice as likely to produce top-box profit
  growth. For us: the home page builds the brand with one feeling, care for
  what a family drinks; the shop and the quiz activate with facts and one
  verb. Do not mix the two jobs in one block.
- **Reeves, Reality in Advertising (1961).** One proposition: an offer to the
  customer, one competitors do not make, strong enough to move people. Ours
  is that the system is chosen and tuned for New York water by people who
  drink it. Every page can carry it; no page needs a second one.
- **Bernbach.** Honesty and respect for the reader sold the Volkswagen to
  America with "Think small" and a photograph. Plain words, a true claim, no
  shouting. If a line would embarrass us read aloud to the customer, it is
  not Bernbach and it does not ship.

None of these overrides section 1 to 7. They answer the question the first
shelf leaves open: how far to go, how plain to be, how often to repeat.

## The check, before any line ships

1. Is there an adjective doing the work a fact should do? Replace it.
2. Does it rank our own products, or name a tier? Remove it.
3. Is every number traceable? If not, it goes on the register or it goes.
4. Would a reader who scans the first five words get the point?
5. Is it half the length of the first draft?
6. Does it use the same word for the same thing as every other page?
7. Could a careful person say it aloud to a customer without wincing?
